Why a different kind of assurance?

A control can operate correctly.
The conclusion can still be wrong.

Policies, approvals and evaluation scores matter. But the records proving that a check occurred are not always evidence that it could detect the failure you care about. Our engagement looks beyond control presence and procedural compliance to the adequacy of the evidence and the reliability of the overall human–AI chain.

Process integrity

Did the safeguards operate?

What was retrieved, evaluated, reviewed, approved or escalated—and what do the records actually establish?

Evidential validity

Was the right evidence examined?

Could a missing document, incomplete sample or misleading proxy leave a material error invisible to every subsequent check?

End-to-end reliability

Does the combination hold?

Do automation, evaluation and human oversight work together against consequential failures under the conditions in which people actually use them?

Consider an omitted annex. An AI reviews a contract without receiving a material annex. Citations resolve, automated checks pass, and a reviewer approves the summary. The recorded steps may be valid while the recommendation is materially wrong. The question is: which safeguard was capable of detecting what was never supplied?

The broader argument is developed in The AI Assurance Halo: What “Validation Passed” Does and Doesn’t Do →. It informs the questions we ask, rather than proving that any particular client control is ineffective.

The basis of our judgement

Research suggests where to look.
Your evidence determines what we can say.

We combine two perspectives. One is a maintained body of research on known and emerging GenAI failure mechanisms and mitigations. The other is the actual operating record of the workflow under review. We test whether findings from the first are applicable to the second, rather than substituting general research for client-specific evidence.

Research-informed challenge

What mechanisms could matter here?

Research into automation bias, source omission, evaluator dependence, reviewer effectiveness and human–AI collaboration can reveal questions ordinary control testing overlooks.

  • Which mechanism might undermine a claimed safeguard?
  • Under what conditions would that research transfer to this workflow?
  • What evidence would support or disprove the concern?
The client's operating evidence

What actually happens in this workflow?

We examine the relevant workflow design, configurations, evaluation data, samples, incidents, logs and reviewer practices, preserving the basis for each material judgement.

  • What was tested—and what was outside the sample?
  • Which operational records support the management claim?
  • Where are we still relying on assumption or incomplete coverage?

The distinction is important.

Research may indicate that visible quality scores encourage reviewer over-reliance. That is a reason to inspect the review interface and test error detection. It does not establish that your reviewers are ineffective. We seek the narrowest conclusion that your own evidence supports.

Where we concentrate attention

We look at the joins.
Not just the controls in isolation.

Individually well-run safeguards can share assumptions, data sources or blind spots. We examine the handoffs where confidence is transferred from one control to the next—and ask what might escape them all.

Source → analysis

What never entered the evidence set?

An absent annex, unrepresentative evaluation sample or outdated retrieved passage may escape checks that operate only on what they receive.

AI analysis → evaluator

An evaluator can share the generator's model family, selected sources or assumptions. A healthy score does not automatically demonstrate an independent challenge.

Can evaluation catch the specific error the system might make?
Evaluation → human review

Is oversight effective or ceremonial?

An approval record confirms a decision occurred; it does not measure what reviewers notice under realistic workload, expertise and source-access conditions.

What disappears in the summary?

A positive management headline can conceal an unresolved exception, incomplete population coverage or a qualification that mattered to the original assessment.

Do material limits survive the trip to the decision-maker?

Looking for blind spots is not assuming failure. The same review can identify controls that appear well designed, show why they work, and recommend only the testing needed to establish their effectiveness.

How an engagement works

An early diagnosis first.
Deeper scrutiny where it matters.

We start with one consequential workflow and its intended decision, not an enterprise-wide compliance inventory. The initial pass helps us and your team identify where the real questions lie. Detailed evidence review and targeted testing follow the risks, rather than a rigid sequence of approvals.

01 · Orientation

Understand the claims and the evidence

Define the workflow boundary and material failure, examine available controls and records, and form an initial working view of what appears supported, disputed or unknown.

Outcome: a focused assurance hypothesis—not a premature verdict.
02 · Challenge

Investigate the consequential joins

Use applicable research to choose targeted samples, independence checks, incident reviews or realistic reviewer tests. Request the smallest additional evidence likely to change the assessment.

Outcome: tested concerns, bounded findings and practical options.
03 · Decision support

State what reliance is justified

Distinguish demonstrated effectiveness from procedural compliance, preserve material qualifications, and agree proportionate strengthening actions before the assurance position is used for an important decision.

Outcome: an evidence-based advisory conclusion, not certification.
When new information arrives

The review can evolve without beginning again.

A later incident log, changed prompt, evaluation result or meeting note may affect a particular diagnosis or recommendation. We trace the affected claims and assumptions and revisit those conclusions, keeping the earlier basis and decisions inspectable.

  1. Record the new information and its provenance.
  2. Identify the specific evidence, controls and conclusions it may affect.
  3. Re-examine the material questions, with additional research or testing where useful.
  4. Update advice with a clear explanation of what changed and why.
Illustrative review question

“A human approves every response.”
What does that actually assure?

Consider a support assistant that generates responses from an approved knowledge base. A quality evaluator scores each response and a support agent must approve it before sending. Management describes the combined checks as strong protection against material factual errors.

What may already be established

Approval logs support the proposition that a human send gate operates. Model and retrieval configurations show how the workflow is intended to work. Neither alone establishes that material inaccuracies are regularly caught.

What we might investigate next

Are reviewers given the underlying source passage? Do the evaluator and generator share assumptions? What happens on a poorly represented document class? How often do seeded material errors get detected under realistic conditions?

This is an illustrative scenario, not a client case study. The correct conclusion might be that the safeguards are effective, need targeted strengthening, or remain insufficiently evidenced. The point of the engagement is to distinguish those outcomes—not to assume one.

What an engagement produces

Clarity about what to trust.
And what to test next.

The emphasis is decision support, not a maturity score. The scope and depth are agreed for the workflow and the decision at hand; the output can range from an initial diagnostic view to a more detailed, evidence-supported advisory report.
