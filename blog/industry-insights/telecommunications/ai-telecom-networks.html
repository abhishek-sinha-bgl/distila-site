AI in Telecom Networks: What the Evidence Shows

AI is changing how networks use spectrum, process signals, manage resources and deliver services. The evidence reveals different capabilities, different levels of maturity and no single engineering path.

26 November 2026 · Epistamate Insights · Abhishek Sinha · ORCID

A telecommunications network is much more than the physical infrastructure through which information travels. The ability to extract usable information from imperfect radio signals, allocate scarce spectrum among competing demands, route traffic, predict failures and deliver a specified quality of service defines the work of the industry. These capabilities have evolved through decades of advances in mathematics, computing, engineering and automation. AI is now being applied to some of the core problems of telecommunications, including the way signals are processed and the way resources are allocated across the network.

Part 1 identified a distinction between carrying more traffic and expanding what an operator can economically offer. It also considered the importance of shared technical capabilities and interoperability to the industry. That distinction applies inside the network. The evidence considered here is most tangible in improvements to established functions, including radio resource allocation and energy management on existing infrastructure. More radical changes to signal processing have promising experimental results, while network sensing and agentic service assurance are at earlier stages. The engineering question is where new methods improve quality or cost, and where they make a different kind of service feasible.

Building on decades of network intelligence

In a mobile network, radio resources must be continually shared among users whose locations, applications and operating conditions are changing. Interference, obstructions, movement and variations in demand make this a complex engineering problem. Modern networks already use sophisticated techniques to estimate channel conditions, adapt how information is encoded, allocate resources, manage movement between cells and respond to varying loads. The use of predictive techniques, feedback loops and automation is not a consequence of the emergence of generative AI.

The history of energy optimisation illustrates the point. A May 2011 technical report from ETSI, based on 3GPP Release 9 describes self-organising network (SON) use cases including the ability to switch off capacity-enhancing cells when their capacity is not required and reactivate them as demand returns. The industry understood the energy-saving mechanism well before the recent advances in AI. Automated fault monitoring and service provisioning similarly predate recent AI advances.

In March 2025, Vodafone UK and Ericsson reported reductions of up to 33% in daily power consumption of 5G radio units at selected London trial sites. Their approach combined traffic prediction, deep sleep, orchestration of cell sleep and performance monitoring. For an operator, reducing radio-unit energy consumption could be economically significant. The figure is a selected-site maximum; network-wide savings, performance against a tuned conventional sleep regime and net implementation costs remain to be established.

The relevant baseline is the performance of established methods pursuing the same engineering objective. A new method need not fundamentally change a network architecture or create a new product to matter commercially. Extracting more capacity from existing assets, reducing energy consumption or improving quality can itself be a substantial source of value.

Learning how to receive a signal

One area of research goes to the physical layer of the network. When a device transmits a signal, the receiver must recover the original information despite the effects of noise, interference and the physical environment. Conventional receivers use mathematical models and signal processing to estimate the radio channel and reconstruct the transmitted information. Learned receivers seek to use patterns in data to perform some of these functions, or to improve the performance of established algorithms.

In an over-the-air experiment, a neural receiver trained for its particular radio environment achieved nearly four times the uplink throughput of the selected conventional receiver at one non-line-of-sight position, where an obstruction blocked the direct radio path. Chen and colleagues built a digital representation of the environment using a radar scan and generated 28,671 simulated channel records to train the receiver for 5G New Radio.

Tests at five positions used the existing 5G waveform. The site-trained receiver had the highest mean uplink throughput of the three tested methods at each position. At the difficult non-line-of-sight position, the rates were 16.2 Mb/s for the site-trained receiver, 4.1 Mb/s for the conventional receiver and 2.4 Mb/s for a neural receiver trained on standardised channel models. (Chen et al., November 2026, Figure 6.)

The generically trained neural receiver performed worse than the conventional receiver. That contrast suggests that knowledge of the actual environment matters considerably. The experiments primarily concern single-input, single-output conditions, using one transmit and one receive antenna. Generalisation to more complex antenna systems remains open, as does the cost of real-time inference.

An ETH Zurich study published in September 2026 found that site-specific fine-tuning substantially improved several learned receivers, with benefits persisting across measurement campaigns separated by more than six months. Yet the lowest error rate in its datasets was achieved by combining site-specific conventional channel estimation with iterative detection and decoding. The studies use different arrangements and measures, so they cannot be ranked directly. The shared implication is that adaptation to the physical environment can matter more than whether a technique is labelled AI.

For an operator, this distinction has practical consequences. A learned approach might improve reception in environments where existing methods struggle, allowing better use of installed spectrum and equipment. On the other hand, producing and maintaining training data for individual locations, validating model behaviour when those locations change, and executing the model under strict timing constraints may introduce costs. A conventional receiver adapted to the same environment may sometimes offer a better engineering trade-off. The commercial opportunity lies in identifying where the improvement is meaningful and transferable, rather than assuming that every network should adopt a learned receiver.

There is also evidence of learning entering existing commercial networks through more focused mechanisms. In August 2026, SoftBank and Ericsson reported testing an AI-enabled link-adaptation scheduler on a commercial 5G network. Link adaptation determines, among other things, how aggressively information can be encoded and transmitted given changing radio conditions. The trial indicated gains of up to 25% in spectral efficiency and 50% in downlink user throughput relative to the conventional link-adaptation method used in the trial. Across the evaluated locations, the companies reported average improvements of about 10% on both measures. The results remain specific to those trial locations. Extracting more usable capacity from spectrum already held by the operator could be economically significant, particularly where the alternative involves adding sites or acquiring spectrum.

As research extends learning to functions at the air interface, the standards process matters: mobile networks need compatible devices and equipment from different suppliers. The Release 19 work and preceding studies have considered AI-assisted beam management, positioning and channel information prediction. Defining interoperable support for these functions is distinct from standardising the models that implement them. Research into AI-native 6G is not a deployed standard. More immediate opportunities can arise from improving internal network functions without requiring changes to the devices customers already use.

Competing architectures for the radio access network

The physical location of computation and the type of hardware used are important determinants of network economics. The radio access network (RAN) connects devices to the rest of the operator’s network and must execute certain functions within stringent timing and reliability requirements. AI adds new types of computation, but the appropriate way to incorporate them is not settled.

Three distinct propositions are often grouped under AI-RAN (artificial intelligence in or alongside the radio access network). Learning can improve network functions such as scheduling and resource allocation. Network functions and other AI workloads can share computing infrastructure. Finally, operators may use distributed computing resources associated with their networks to offer AI services to customers. The economics differ. Improving a radio function can be valuable without any external AI workload. Offering AI services, however, depends on actual demand at particular locations and on the alternatives considered in Part 1, including remote cloud processing, improved model efficiency and computing on customer devices. Shared infrastructure is only advantageous if it meets the requirements of both kinds of workload at an attractive cost.

One route is to obtain more from installed equipment. Ericsson’s AI in RAN software subscription, introduced in June 2026, provides models designed to execute on compatible existing radio and baseband hardware (which processes digital radio signals). It may extend capabilities without a broad hardware replacement programme. The continuing licence cost, model development and operational data arrangements affect how much of the engineering benefit translates into operator economics. Software licensing is not new to telecommunications, but the ability to validate, update and replace models becomes an additional operational consideration.

Another route draws on technologies developed for the wider computing ecosystem. As discussed in Part 1, Nokia’s AI and cloud customers are becoming an increasingly important source of growth. Nokia and NVIDIA are developing architectures based on general-purpose accelerated computing, potentially supporting radio processing and AI application workloads together. Nokia’s October 2026 announcement describes flexible configurations supporting network software alongside inference. Specialised radio software and accelerated computing need not be mutually exclusive: in March 2026, T-Mobile and Ericsson demonstrated Ericsson’s Cloud RAN software on NVIDIA infrastructure. Different environments may warrant different architectures.

Specialised hardware may offer favourable energy and cost characteristics for predictable real-time functions, while general computing resources could provide greater flexibility. If the anticipated external workloads do not materialise, an operator could be paying for idle computing capability. If they do, the network must still guarantee its own processing requirements and isolate workloads. As with fibre investment discussed in Part 1, an architectural commitment should be considered in relation to the durability of its assumptions and the alternatives available if those assumptions change. Equipment, licensing, energy, cooling, upgrade paths, migration and the ability to reuse resources or change suppliers all enter the comparison.

Openness and interoperability matter in this context, but should not be confused with AI itself. Open RAN is concerned with enabling greater separation and interoperability of network components and interfaces. It may increase the options available to operators implementing intelligent control, although the introduction and integration of new suppliers also have costs. The appropriate measure of its contribution is not the number of open interfaces, but whether it gives the operator useful architectural and commercial flexibility while meeting the required service quality and economics.

Shared standards also influence how long an engineering advantage can remain distinctive. The ITU’s IMT-2030 framework identifies interoperability as an objective for the next generation of mobile systems. A learned method may give an early adopter a performance advantage, particularly if it can exploit local network conditions or implement the technique better than competitors. If the method subsequently becomes available from equipment suppliers across the industry, the initial advantage may narrow. Common interfaces and compatible devices can also enlarge the market for new services. Neither proprietary implementation nor openness guarantees differentiation; the enduring question is what quality, cost and service capabilities the operator can deliver through its chosen implementation.

Where should an AI application be processed?

The potential to use distributed network infrastructure for computing is relevant beyond the radio interface. In many conventional cloud architectures, information is collected at a device or business customer site and sent to a remote computing facility for processing. Some applications can tolerate the time this takes; others may need more predictable and rapid responses. A network operator could offer not only connectivity but also access to suitably located computing resources and the ability to assure performance between them and the customer.

A study by Hariz Yet and colleagues examined this question using a 5G standalone AI-RAN testbed with device, edge and remote-cloud computing tiers. For one quantised 7-billion-parameter model (the AWQ variant, which reduces the precision of model weights), 99% of requests processed at the RAN edge met a half-second deadline, compared with 32.9% at the remote cloud on the tested route. Other model configurations performed considerably worse at the edge. For a one-second deadline, all evaluated cloud configurations met the target in the reported measurements. The experiment shows why model choice can matter as much as the location of computing resources. (February 2026 author preprint, Table IV).

The experiment also examined sharing computing infrastructure with baseband processing under traffic load. With fixed partitioning of accelerator resources, the researchers maintained the measured timing and stability indicators for baseband processing while serving up to twenty concurrent inference clients. The result concerns the testbed’s measured timing and stability indicators and partitioned resources; commercial-scale workload combinations and deployment economics remain open.

The findings qualify the commercial proposition. An application with a tight deadline might benefit from a particular combination of model, network and computing location. A less demanding application might obtain equally satisfactory performance from a remote cloud. Future devices may be capable of handling some tasks locally. An operator could help customers choose computing locations and models based on actual workloads and performance requirements. Simply installing computing capacity close to a mobile site is not the same thing as offering a differentiated service.

Capacity without new fibre

AI demand is also influencing optical transport. The Optical Internetworking Forum’s 800ZR agreement of October 2024 defines an interoperable 800 Gbps coherent interface (using the phase and amplitude of light to carry data) for particular data centre interconnection distances. In 2026, Telxius and Nokia reported deploying compact optical transceiver modules to expand capacity over existing terrestrial infrastructure. These are advances in conventional optical engineering rather than AI methods, but the growing demand for AI computing increases their relevance. They offer operators another way to meet demand. As discussed in Part 1, on suitable routes, improving transmission equipment can increase the capacity of existing fibre and offer an alternative to new construction. Reach, installed fibre characteristics and other route constraints determine the applicability of that option.

A network that can do more than communicate

Integrated sensing and communication could extend a network’s role beyond connecting devices. A radio signal interacts with its physical environment, and information extracted from reflections can reveal the presence or movement of objects that are not connected to the network. This builds on established radar and signal-processing techniques, using communications infrastructure as a possible additional source of sensing information.

In July 2026, AT&T and Ericsson demonstrated network-based drone detection over a 5G network near a stadium, reporting detection, location and tracking of multiple drones. In November, Ericsson announced its integrated sensing system, combining sensing-capable radios, edge computing, signal processing and AI. Initial sensing-only commercial pilots were planned for 2027, with broader deployments envisaged from 2028. The evidence is at the demonstration stage, with performance across environments and economics relative to dedicated sensors still to be established.

The potential change is nevertheless distinctive. An operator’s existing sites and network coverage might become relevant to activities other than connecting devices. Dedicated sensing equipment could be complemented by information from communications infrastructure, with the operator offering a sensing or monitoring capability to other organisations. The commercial case would depend on coverage, detection reliability, false alarms, permissions, who integrates the information into customer operations and what additional equipment and processing are needed. A complementary sensing service can be useful without replacing dedicated sensors, provided its incremental contribution is reliable and economical.

Other network capabilities can extend the value of a communications service. Part 1 discussed how telecommunications signals can complement banking data in fraud prevention, and how AI can translate conversations within a telephone call. These examples involve different combinations of existing information, computing and distribution. In one, the operator supplies an additional signal to a customer’s decision process. In another, it changes the experience of communication itself. Both involve engineering and operational questions about how the service can be delivered reliably, with acceptable latency, privacy and accuracy. They should not be treated as equivalent AI use cases or assumed to create the same economics.

From optimising components to assuring a service

A recurring issue across these developments is that improvements in individual network functions do not necessarily improve the service experienced by a customer. A faster radio connection may still be constrained by transport capacity, computing queues or an application that cannot process the information quickly enough. Saving energy by switching off capacity could create a problem if the network cannot restore it fast enough when demand changes. Optimising individual components is essential, but there may be additional value in coordinating decisions across them.

In telecommunications, established operational and business support systems already provide monitoring, provisioning, billing and service assurance. The introduction of agents or learning-based controllers should be assessed in relation to those functions. One possible development is that the cost of assembling information across multiple systems, interpreting its significance and deciding how to intervene falls sufficiently to enable more frequent or more granular coordination. This could change the economics of assuring a customer’s service, particularly where delivering it requires several different capabilities to work together.

Early experiments are testing coordination across network functions. In May 2026, Wu and colleagues presented a hierarchical agent-native network architecture, combining higher-level planning with local operational actions. Wu and colleagues reported in their 5G core experiments a reduction of 86% in mean time to repair in the tested scenarios. In November 2026, AIS and Huawei announced a joint innovation centre in Thailand to explore agent-based autonomy across network domains. These represent laboratory evidence and an operator-supplier innovation programme, not autonomous operation across a production network.

Service assurance illustrates why coordination could be commercially consequential. Business customers have long purchased private networks and other services with commitments on latency, reliability or packet loss. The Dynamic 5G offer described in Part 1 provides a more granular performance commitment. If an operator could observe and verify network conditions, provision resources, predict service degradation and intervene at substantially lower cost, it might become feasible to extend performance commitments to more customers or shorter periods. The economics depend on the cost of assurance, reserved capacity, failures and customer-side dependencies, compared with established automation and service agreements. This is a potential expansion of the commercial opportunity set, not a demonstrated effect of AI on the existing product.

Telecommunications operators also have critical-infrastructure obligations. The Australian Communications and Media Authority (ACMA) imposed penalties on Optus in 2024 over failures associated with emergency calling during its 2023 outage. The outage, unrelated to AI, underscores the stakes of changes to critical network operations. A system able to act across several network domains might coordinate recovery more effectively, but an incorrect action could also have wider consequences. The scope of autonomous authority, conditions for human intervention, reliable fallback and the ability to reverse changes are part of the engineering problem.

What changes for the telecommunications operator?

The evidence is strongest for bounded improvements to individual network functions: power management and link adaptation have been tested on commercial infrastructure. Learned receivers and distributed inference show measurable advantages in particular experimental environments, but also reveal the importance of conventional alternatives and the cost of deployment. Sensing and agentic multi-domain assurance indicate possible extensions of network capability, with much more limited evidence of sustained commercial outcomes. For network and business leaders, the practical questions begin with which network constraints matter most and which methods address them most effectively. An improvement to the cost of delivering an established service can be just as meaningful as a new product. The test includes sustained performance against a competent conventional baseline, the cost of software and compute, operational reliability and the ability to maintain or change the technology as the network evolves.

The next question is which combinations of capabilities expand what can be offered economically. Could an existing network provide performance commitments to a customer that previously needed dedicated resources? Could its information or sensing capabilities contribute to a service offered in cooperation with other operators or industries? Common standards and interfaces may make those propositions viable at scale, while the implementation, service integration and operational quality distinguish the operator. Control of underlying technology and operational decisions matters, but exclusivity is not necessarily the source of value.

The most mature examples in this investigation concern selective improvements to existing functions and assets. More substantial changes in reception, computing architecture and the role of the network are credible avenues of development, with different maturity and economics. The opportunity is to improve service quality and reduce delivery costs, and to discover which new capabilities justify different service commitments. Sustained gains, interoperable implementation and the ability to deliver the resulting service reliably will determine how far those opportunities translate into competitive advantage.

Questions about the evidence

Questions arising from the evidence

What does AI-RAN mean?

It can mean using AI to improve radio network functions, sharing computing infrastructure between radio and AI workloads, or offering AI services from distributed network computing resources. Each has different performance requirements and economics.

Is AI always better than conventional telecommunications engineering?

No. The receiver experiments illustrate how learning and adaptation to local conditions can improve performance. Another study found that a site-adapted conventional receiver achieved the lowest error rate in its datasets. The engineering problem and quality of the comparator matter.

Does AI inference need to run at the network edge?

Not universally. The testbed described in this article shows that model choice and computing location both influence whether application deadlines are met. A cloud or device may be adequate for a less demanding workload.

How close are telecommunications networks to becoming autonomous?

Network automation is longstanding. Agentic coordination across network domains remains at varied stages of research and testing; the examples here do not establish safe, sustained network-wide autonomy. Authority, fallback and reversal controls matter alongside performance.
