AI in Telecommunications: Changing Markets, Services and Strategy

Changing customer requirements, emerging competitors and new service capabilities are reshaping the commercial opportunities and strategic choices available to telecommunications operators.

26 November 2026 · Epistamate Insights · Abhishek Sinha · ORCID

Successive generations of telecommunications technology have changed what customers can do, how services are delivered and where commercial value resides. Mobile broadband enabled entirely new businesses, while much of the resulting growth in revenue occurred beyond the networks carrying their traffic. AI presents a related but distinct challenge: it changes what customers require from networks, what operators can economically offer and the competitive environment in which they operate. This article follows those changes into the business choices operators face. Part 2 considers the evolution of the engineering and operational capabilities on which those choices depend.

The immediate efficiency benefits of AI attract much attention. For telecommunications, however, the larger question concerns the business itself. Greater traffic is one opportunity; a service that can be delivered at a different cost, to a different customer or with a new performance commitment is another. The significance of each depends on how operators respond, the capabilities they develop or access, and the economics of their choices.

A familiar challenge, but not a universal outcome

The experience of the internet economy offers a starting point. A UK government review of the mobile market reports that mobile traffic grew by 495% between 2018 and 2025. Over a separate period, from 2019 to 2024, average data consumption nearly tripled while mobile service prices fell by 25% in real terms. Customers received considerably more for their money. The same review reports that operator income and EBITDA both fell by 18% in real terms from 2019 to 2024.

Internet-based messaging and calling have displaced much of the traffic traditionally charged by message or minute. Businesses can use software to coordinate networks built over ordinary internet connections rather than relying exclusively on dedicated private network services. The GSMA’s 2022 Internet Value Chain study documented a declining relative share of ecosystem revenue for connectivity as online services grew more rapidly. The industry-sponsored estimates describe ecosystem revenue shares; they cannot establish a transfer of economic profit.

There is a structural reason why technological progress need not translate into lasting exclusivity for an individual operator. The commercial success of a new mobile generation depends on a common ecosystem of devices, equipment, networks and services. The ITU’s framework for IMT-2030, the basis for the development of 6G standards, identifies interoperability across systems as a design objective. An operator may be first to deploy a new capability, but compatible devices and widespread industry adoption are integral to its value. This does not imply unrestricted access to competing networks, or that all operators will provide the same quality of service. Spectrum, coverage, reliability, implementation quality and cost can still distinguish them. The underlying standard, however, is unlikely by itself to confer lasting exclusivity.

The experiences of individual markets show why connectivity should not be treated as a uniformly low-growth utility. T-Mobile US increased its 5G broadband customer base from 6.43 million at the end of 2024 to 8.45 million at the end of 2025, including customers acquired through the UScellular transaction. Existing wireless infrastructure was being used to compete in home broadband. In India, Reliance reported FY2026 digital services operating revenue growth of 14.2% and EBITDA growth of 17.8%. Conventional technology, distribution and market reach remain commercially consequential; neither set of results isolates a contribution from newer AI. (T-Mobile FY2025 results; Reliance FY2026 annual report.)

The industry’s challenge is not to move away from connectivity but to distinguish growth in traffic from the value of a service and the returns earned on its delivery. Common technical capabilities can enlarge the market for everyone; differentiation depends on how they are implemented, combined with other capabilities and delivered to customers. AI should be considered against that background, rather than assumed to bring either another missed opportunity or a new era of exceptional returns.

When advances in AI change the demand for networks

AI creates demand for telecommunications infrastructure, but it also changes the characteristics of that demand. The connections between accelerators within a computing facility, the links between geographically separated facilities, the networks carrying business customer requests and the access networks serving individual devices have different requirements. An operator providing long-distance fibre does not participate in every one of these markets merely because it is supplying connectivity to AI developers.

Consider distributed training. When the computing resources needed for training a model are spread across facilities, those resources need to exchange information as the training progresses. Connecting facilities with high-capacity, low-latency links is one way to enable the computation. NVIDIA’s Spectrum-XGS announcement in August 2025 explicitly targets this architecture, allowing operators of AI infrastructure to connect geographically distributed data centres.

At the same time, research is changing the computation itself. In April 2026, Google DeepMind published results from Decoupled DiLoCo, an approach in which separate computing clusters perform more of their work independently before exchanging information. In one simulation involving eight data centres, the required bandwidth fell from 198 Gbps for conventional data-parallel training to 0.84 Gbps for the alternative method. Separately, the researchers reported training a 12-billion-parameter model across four US regions using wide-area links of 2 to 5 Gbps. The two results are not the same experiment, and neither establishes that the approach will replace high-bandwidth networks for frontier-scale training. They do demonstrate that the algorithms can materially alter the communications requirement. (DeepMind research announcement and Figure 2; research paper.)

From a telecommunications perspective, there are at least two plausible consequences. Reducing the required bandwidth between facilities could weaken demand for some premium dedicated connections. However, if the same advances enable training across facilities that could not previously have participated economically, the number of locations requiring connectivity could grow. Both could happen. The value of a route could depend on what kinds of workloads its customers run, how those workloads evolve and whether other customers can use the capacity.

Contracted demand changes the investment question

Lumen has been repositioning itself around the combination of physical fibre infrastructure, programmable connectivity and complementary digital services. In November 2026 it reported cumulative signed Private Connectivity Fabric agreements exceeding $13.5 billion, including contracts to support AI infrastructure. Its third-quarter recognised revenue from that category was $66 million. The signed value covers multi-year agreements, rather than recognised revenue or realised profit. Meanwhile, strategic business revenues grew 9.6% year on year but legacy business revenues declined 17.2%, leaving total business revenues 4.5% lower. (Lumen quarterly results; Q3 SEC-filed presentation, slide 16.)

Lumen’s approach brings the historical question of who funds network expansion into sharper focus. Investment made in anticipation of aggregate traffic growth differs from construction supported by specific customer commitments. Lumen describes customer-funded construction and ongoing service arrangements; contract duration, funding terms, residual routes and capacity, and recurring income could materially affect the operator’s exposure. Such arrangements are not unprecedented, and the full terms and returns are undisclosed.

The reported growth in strategic services alongside declining legacy revenues shows a transition in progress, rather than either a demonstrated failure or a completed transformation. Lumen’s programmable network strategy also seeks to change the services it provides over its fibre infrastructure. Its Q3 Form 10-Q identifies the risk of misjudging the timing, scale or nature of AI demand. Developments such as DiLoCo help explain why the timing, scale and nature of demand all matter.

Demand for inference presents a different commercial question. Some applications, such as industrial systems responding to changing physical conditions, could benefit from computing close to where data is generated. For others, time spent processing the request within the model may be more consequential than network latency. Improvements to models or devices can also make some tasks possible locally. The opportunity is not to locate computing indiscriminately at network sites, but to identify workloads requiring a combination of connectivity, processing and assured performance that is difficult to obtain elsewhere. Part 2 examines the experimental evidence for these alternatives.

Customers, competitors and suppliers are changing roles

Some of the largest customers of telecommunications operators are also increasingly building their own communications infrastructure. According to the OECD’s 2026 assessment of connectivity, content providers wholly owned or participated in operating consortia for more than 60% of the global submarine cable kilometres entering, or expected to enter, service between 2024 and 2027. The comparable figure for 2010 to 2015 was 8%. Technology companies accounted for nearly three-quarters of international submarine bandwidth demand in 2024. These are different measures, and the ownership figure is for new cable kilometres, including planned deployments, not the entire installed network.

This shift was underway well before the current AI wave. Its relevance now is that growing AI demand does not necessarily strengthen the bargaining position of the operator supplying it. A customer with the scale, expertise and capital to construct its own network can decide which capabilities to own and which to procure. Where an operator has scarce routes, rights of way, local relationships or existing infrastructure that can be put to use quickly, buying from the operator may still be attractive.

Telstra’s relationship with Microsoft illustrates the coexistence of these roles. In August 2024, Microsoft signed contracts for the initial routes on Telstra’s new intercity fibre network, while Telstra announced an investment in Microsoft Copilot licences for its own workforce. This was a reciprocal commercial relationship involving very different capabilities. A hyperscaler can be both a customer and a supplier, even as it develops infrastructure that competes with an operator’s offerings. The net economics of the relationship are undisclosed. (Telstra and Microsoft, August 2024.)

The suppliers of telecommunications operators are also responding to new sources of demand. Nokia’s third-quarter 2026 results reported €631 million of sales to AI and cloud customers, compared with €279 million a year earlier, a 123% increase on a constant-currency basis. Sales to telecommunications providers were approximately €3.64 billion, essentially unchanged on that basis. The traditional telecommunications market is still substantially larger, but growth from a different set of customers is influencing the company’s strategic priorities. Nokia’s October 2025 announcement of a strategic partnership and planned $1 billion equity investment from NVIDIA explicitly connected its future radio access technology with opportunities in AI infrastructure. (Nokia Q3 2026 report; SEC filing, customer-category table; Nokia strategic announcement.)

Operators could benefit from technologies developed for the larger computing ecosystem, while the growth of a different customer base may also influence supplier priorities. Part 2 considers how this bears on the choice between software upgrades to existing equipment and alternative computing architectures. The question is whether those choices improve performance, cost or flexibility, and what new dependencies they introduce.

What might an operator sell differently?

An operator can respond to AI by carrying more traffic, but it can also reconsider the services it offers. One direction concerns the economics of assuring performance. Telstra’s Dynamic 5G service allows a business customer to specify performance requirements at an agreed location using a dedicated slice of the mobile network. The commercial offer includes hourly performance-linked charging, with additional fees in some circumstances. The commitment covers the designated device to the edge of Telstra’s mobile network, not the performance of the entire customer application.

Selling measurable performance is not a new business model. Telstra’s international IPVPN service schedule, for example, specifies latency, packet delivery and jitter commitments with financial credits for certain breaches. What is potentially different is the granularity, duration and flexibility of the commitment. If the cost of provisioning, monitoring and assuring performance falls sufficiently, an operator may be able to offer short-duration, application-specific services that would previously have required too much manual work or dedicated infrastructure. AI may contribute to that change alongside established network technologies. The economics of reserved capacity, service delivery and actual customer demand still determine whether the proposition works.

A different opportunity arises from information that operators possess because they run communications networks. In Australia, CommBank and Telstra have combined telecommunications signals with the bank’s own information to help identify and intervene in suspicious transactions. In August 2024, CommBank described one instance in which approximately A$70,000 of attempted payments were declined following an intervention. The bank’s account documents an intervention in a specific incident without estimating total fraud losses avoided or operator revenue. The initiative began in 2023 and does not necessarily depend on generative AI.

The scale of such a service depends in part on interoperability. A bank needs to protect customers regardless of which telecommunications network they use. The GSMA Open Gateway initiative extends a familiar industry principle to network-derived services: common interfaces allow application developers to use capabilities across operators. India’s federated network API initiative illustrates this approach. Cooperation can make a proposition more useful while reducing exclusivity or shifting bargaining power to an intermediary. Differentiation may depend less on exclusive possession of a signal than on the quality, integration and reliability of the service built around it, with data rights and privacy integral to the proposition.

An example in which AI changes the communications service itself is T-Mobile’s Live Translation beta, launched in May 2026. Language translation is integrated into ordinary telephone calls rather than requiring users to subscribe to a separate AI application. Distribution through the network is a potential advantage, but handset and application alternatives may provide comparable capabilities. The beta is an example of product development, not yet evidence of enduring differentiation or monetisation.

By contrast, distributing third-party AI subscriptions extends an established commercial model. Airtel offered eligible customers a year of Perplexity Pro, while Jio offered eighteen months of Google AI Pro. As early as 2018, Airtel’s Netflix partnership provided selected mobile postpaid and home broadband customers with a temporary subscription benefit and integrated billing. Such over-the-top (OTT) subscription bundles are routine in India and also occur in other markets.

The updated Airtel terms reveal a dependency: after a global policy change by Perplexity, certain previously enrolled customers were required to provide payment details to retain their free benefit. Both Airtel’s and Jio’s new-claim windows have ended, with eligible subscriptions continuing for their respective terms. Expiry alone does not demonstrate commercial failure. The operator uses a third-party benefit to differentiate its own subscription. Yet the provider can change the conditions under which customers receive it.

How telecommunications operators are choosing to respond

Beyond specific product developments, operators are making different decisions about the scope of their businesses. Their established capabilities, markets, capital requirements and customer relationships help explain why no single response follows from the same technological developments.

Telstra has been simplifying its business technology services portfolio while investing in infrastructure and differentiated networking. Its FY2026 results presentation describes planned strategic investment of around A$1.8 billion through FY2028, with a targeted mid-teens internal rate of return. It also agreed to transfer majority ownership of its expanded Versent business to Infosys, retaining a minority interest; the transaction was still pending according to Infosys’s September 2026 interim filing.

Singtel has expanded through NCS in Australian technology services markets that overlap with Telstra’s, including its 2022 acquisition of Dialog Group. Its FY2026 annual report also describes investment in regional digital infrastructure and AI computing, supported by partners and external capital. The contrasting choices predate or extend beyond AI. They show that owning a capability, retaining access to it and bearing the capital risk are separate strategic decisions.

Some operators are also pursuing opportunities in AI computing. Deutsche Telekom reported in November 2026 that capacity in the initial expansion phase of its Munich industrial AI cloud was sold out and announced further investment. China Mobile reported RMB89.8 billion of computing-services revenue in 2025. The scale, customer relationships and domestic market contexts differ considerably. Neither disclosure establishes standalone AI computing profitability, but both show actual commercial activity extending beyond conventional connectivity. (Deutsche Telekom, November 2026; China Mobile 2025 annual results.)

Strategic choices are shaped by regulation as well as capabilities and capital. Not every opportunity is available to every operator. In Australia, the competition tribunal upheld rejection of the Telstra–TPG regional network-sharing arrangement in 2023; the regulator did not oppose a different Optus–TPG arrangement in 2024. Market position and the details of the proposal mattered. The non-AI case illustrates that technical feasibility and commercial attractiveness do not guarantee permission to proceed. (ACCC, 2023; ACCC, 2024.)

Commitments in a changing environment

Infrastructure commitments can be affected by changes in algorithms, the geography of demand and alternative ways of delivering connectivity. The distributed training research discussed earlier illustrates the first: even when aggregate demand for AI rises, a particular workload’s communications requirements can change. The other two originate at least partly outside AI itself.

Where computing facilities can be built

The geography of AI demand can change for reasons that have little to do with the technology. Data centres require power, cooling, land, political acceptance and regulatory approvals. Local opposition to development can change which sites are economically or politically feasible. Ballotpedia’s analysis of the November 2026 US elections found restrictive outcomes in the overwhelming majority of the local data centre ballot measures it tracked. The tracked contests provide evidence of local opposition, without measuring nationwide public sentiment. (Ballotpedia, 2026 data centre measures.)

In Canada, TELUS withdrew from two proposed Vancouver AI data centre projects in November 2026 while continuing the expansion of its existing Kamloops facility. The developer, Westbank, indicated that it intended to proceed with the Vancouver projects with a different partner. Local opposition had been reported, but TELUS did not publicly attribute its decision to that opposition. The change is nevertheless a reminder that the original configuration of a multi-site development may not survive. (Data Center Dynamics, November 2026.) If facilities are relocated, connectivity requirements may move with them. The consequences for a particular operator will depend on the routes, contracts and investments already committed.

New entrants and alternative infrastructure

Not every development that changes the competitive environment will originate in AI. Satellite broadband is an example. Starlink initially addressed many locations where terrestrial access was inadequate or expensive. In its spring 2026 connectivity update, Ofcom attributed a reduction in the number of UK premises lacking affordable basic broadband largely to the availability of cheaper satellite services. The expanding service footprint does not yet indicate mass-market migration away from terrestrial networks.

Direct-to-device satellite services introduce another possibility. T-Mobile already offers T-Satellite, using Starlink to provide messaging and selected data applications on compatible mobile phones in locations beyond terrestrial coverage. In May 2026, the US Federal Communications Commission approved, subject to conditions, a proposed transfer of spectrum licences from EchoStar to SpaceX. Its decision (DA 26-471, paragraphs 1 to 4) approves spectrum assignments for next-generation direct-to-device services. The approval increases the scope for future competition; it does not establish equivalence with terrestrial services.

There remain substantial differences in capacity, indoor coverage, economics and spectrum availability. It is unlikely that a satellite proposition suitable for remote areas will immediately become a competitive substitute for dense urban networks. But a new entrant need not replace all of an incumbent’s infrastructure to change the economics of extending coverage or the nature of the customer relationship. An operator may find it attractive to partner with a satellite provider today while recognising that the provider’s own capabilities and commercial options may develop independently.

Google’s reported Project Suncatcher milestone in October 2026, a limited AI response generated on a processor in orbit, points to a longer-horizon possibility: orbital computing, if commercially viable, would change the roles of ground stations, inter-satellite links and terrestrial backhaul.

The industry is accustomed to making long-lived investments in a changing technological and regulatory environment. AI adds further uncertainties because advances in models, training methods, applications and computing architectures can occur over periods much shorter than the life of a fibre route or the duration of a spectrum licence. But uncertainty is not necessarily an argument against investment. Reusable assets, staged commitments, customer financing and a range of potential uses can make an investment attractive even when its original demand assumptions evolve. The importance of these arrangements is not unique to AI, although the range of external developments that can affect them is expanding.

What should telecommunications operators be examining?

The evidence points neither to an inevitable repeat of the internet-era distribution of value nor to a general imperative for operators to become AI companies. Some opportunities arise from customer-backed infrastructure investment; others from changing the services that can be provided over existing assets. Common standards, access to supplier innovation and cooperation across networks can enlarge these possibilities. The competitive challenge is to turn shared capabilities into propositions that are valuable to customers, through quality, coverage, cost, integration or additional services.

For telecommunications leaders, the distinction between more traffic and a new commercial proposition matters. Which customer requirements are sufficiently different to justify a new service? Which existing assets, information and capabilities make the operator a credible provider? Which complementary capabilities should be developed internally or accessed through partners, and where does cooperation increase the value that can be created even if some exclusivity is surrendered?

Existing commitments deserve the same scrutiny. Their economics depend on assumptions about the nature, location and duration of demand, the distribution of risk and the alternative uses of the infrastructure. Advances in algorithms, devices and satellite networks, alongside regulation and customer strategies, can alter those assumptions.

The industry’s evolution also depends on what its networks become capable of doing. Improvements in engineering can change the cost of delivering established services, enable coverage that was previously uneconomic or make a more granular performance commitment feasible. Those possibilities are a source of strategic opportunity even when the underlying technology is shared. Part 2 examines where AI is changing the engineering and operation of networks, how these methods compare with established alternatives, and how far the resulting capabilities can change what operators reliably and economically deliver.

Questions about the evidence

Questions arising from the evidence

Will growing AI demand necessarily increase telecommunications operator profits?

No. Infrastructure costs, the timing and characteristics of demand, competition, contract terms and customer financing all affect the return. Lumen’s contracted demand and simultaneous legacy revenue decline illustrate why revenue growth and retained economic value must be distinguished.

Can operators differentiate themselves when they use common telecommunications standards?

Common standards make compatible devices and services possible at scale. Operators can still differentiate through spectrum, coverage, reliability, implementation quality, cost and service integration. The underlying standard alone rarely provides lasting exclusivity.

Could more efficient AI training methods reduce demand for network infrastructure?

Research such as Decoupled DiLoCo shows that particular distributed training workloads can require far less bandwidth. If such methods become widely applicable, lower requirements per connection may coexist with more locations participating in distributed computing.

Do AI subscription partnerships represent a new telecommunications business model?

They extend familiar digital distribution and subscription-bundling models. The Airtel and Jio examples show why the economics and conditions of a third-party benefit matter; network-native AI services such as translation change the communications service itself.
